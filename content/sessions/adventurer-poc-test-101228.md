The harbor logs would show nothing unusual that day — a ship arrived, a crew disembarked, and the Shardsea of Governance went on conducting its business with the indifferent efficiency of a place that has processed ten thousand arrivals before and expects ten thousand more. But the Compass of Lost Tides had brought them here with purpose, and purpose, in this world, has a way of making itself known whether one is ready or not.

Rides the Wake was the first down the gangplank, as she so often was — not from eagerness exactly, but from the simple fact that stillness never suited her on solid ground. Granit followed with the measured tread of someone cataloguing what they saw, what they smelled, what the stones underfoot suggested about the people who had laid them. Lyvriele committed the dockside to memory the way she committed everything: completely, quietly, without announcing she had done so. Vargr came last, scanning the faces in the crowd with the particular attention of a man who has learned that the dead were once among the living, and the living carry their histories like anchors they cannot see.

The Shardsea of Governance sprawled before them in all its complicated, layered authority. This was not a place of erupting volcanoes or feral spirits or rising tides — the dangers here wore better clothes and spoke in complete sentences and smiled when they meant something else entirely. Somewhere in these waters, among the merchant convoys and naval patrols and the slow machinery of institutional power, something had gone wrong at the level of the systems that held civilization together. The party did not yet know the shape of it. They knew only that the leylines the Compass had shown them — that spreading purple miasma threading through the world's deep architecture — did not stop at the borders of polite society.

There were practical matters first. Rations. Charts. The ship needed tending. These are the unglamorous facts of a life spent moving between catastrophes, and the party had learned by now that neglecting them was how heroes died stupid deaths far from anything that mattered. The port had all of these things, somewhere, behind a sufficient application of coin and patience. And somewhere beyond the provisioning and the paperwork, the Shardsea of Governance waited with whatever lesson it had been holding in trust for them — something about law, perhaps, or legitimacy, or the difference between authority that is earned and authority that is simply enforced.

They had cleared fire and wind and corrupted ocean in turn. They had spoken with the dead and restored broken things and killed what could not be healed. The compass pointed onward. Whatever came next, it would not be simple, and it would not be small, and the party of four — because there were four now, and had been four for long enough that the shape of the group had closed around the absence — moved into the crowd with the practiced ease of people who have already survived enough to know that surviving is not the same as being finished.
