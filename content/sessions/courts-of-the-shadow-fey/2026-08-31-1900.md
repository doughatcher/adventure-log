The lower court of the Shadow Fey palace was not a place that rewarded impatience, and by now the party had learned that much at least. The corridors of the service level smelled of tallow and old stone and something faintly vegetal that no one had yet identified, and the goblins who scurried through them had long since stopped treating the Zobecki strangers as novelties. That, in its own small way, was a form of acceptance.

Aldric's summons before King Mago came without ceremony — a goblin page, a crooked finger, and a walk through low-ceilinged passages to the hall where the goblin king held his abbreviated court. The matter at hand was the wardstone Aldric had shattered with his own fireball on the Shadow Road, a piece of infrastructure he had not known was there until he was already responsible for its ruin. He examined the damaged stone with the careful attention of a man who had spent his whole life reading things other people had written, and what he read in it told him the stone had served as both message-relay and alarm — a fey convenience disguised as ordinary masonry. Ten-Pound Hammer stood nearby with the particular patience of a craftsman waiting to see whether someone else understands the work. Aldric repaired it, and then improved upon the repair, and when Ten-Pound Hammer accepted the result without comment the matter was considered closed. King Mago, apparently satisfied that the man who broke his ward could at least fix it, appointed Aldric Reader of the Lower Court before the morning was out.

The appointment was tested almost immediately. A mute goblin named Billoc had been brought up on a charge of theft, his hands bound behind him in the custom of accused persons awaiting judgment. Aldric looked at the prisoner and saw the problem at once: Billoc could not speak, and Billoc could not sign, and a court that offered him no means of reply was offering him no defense at all. The party made the argument plainly — that a man whose only voice is his hands cannot answer charges while those hands are tied, and that releasing them was not mercy but procedure. Mago heard it, weighed it with the skeptical patience of a king who has adjudicated too many small disputes to be easily moved, and found it sound. Billoc's hands were freed. He signed his innocence; there was no proof against him; he was released. His gratitude was the wordless but unmistakable kind, and he slipped away into the palace corridors leaving the party with the knowledge that somewhere in those passages there was a goblin who owed them something.

Magnolia had made a promise to the kitchens and she kept it. Fifty goodberries, conjured from whatever green memory of sunlight she could reach in this sunless place, were delivered to the head cook, who crushed them into soup without ceremony and without particular thanks — though the lack of thanks in the lower court was itself a kind of professional acknowledgment. Status followed. The lower court noticed people who did what they said they would do.

Tam, meanwhile, had gone to the stables, which suited him better than courtrooms. The Black Prince's hunting dogs were large and suspicious and hungry in the way of dogs that are fed on schedule rather than affection, and Tam addressed both problems with meat and with whatever quality it is that animals recognize in people who have spent time in wild places. By the time he left, the dogs were no longer suspicious. Kolya, the kennel master, was a cautious man with the self-contained manner of someone who has found his place in a complicated world and intends to keep it, but he warmed enough to answer questions. The great blue horse in the far stall was Lazulin, the Black Prince's own mount. The hunt itself was a high-court affair — quarry conjured by Count Samus Hollowgrand, an event that drew attention from well above the service level. It was the kind of thing that could move a person's standing considerably, if they could find their way into it.

Judith and Tam made their way to the guard hall later, where Captain Memorethian's soldiers from Allosis occupied their cots and their patrols with the professional tedium of garrison life. The soldiers were Shadow Fey but not courtiers, and their concerns were territorial rather than political — they guarded the castle because that was what they had been sent to do, and they regarded strangers with the mild suspicion of people who are not paid to be curious. Tam offered to show them his swords and the display landed somewhere between unimpressive and harmless, but the conversation that followed it was more useful than the performance. The party had named themselves as delegates from Zobeck — a city the soldiers had never heard of, which they received as one receives news of any distant satellite: with mild interest and no particular alarm. It was not a connection made, exactly, but it was a door left ajar.

As the day's work settled into the uneasy quiet of the palace's lower hours, the standing of the four of them in King Mago's court was measurably improved. Aldric and Magnolia had climbed to seven; Tam and Judith held at five. The Summer Court continued its session somewhere above them, its music and its politics filtered down through stone and distance into something that felt less like sound than like pressure. The seasonal clock was still running. The path to the Queen remained gated behind layers of status and invitation they did not yet possess. But they had a name — Kolya — and through him, perhaps, the hunt. They had a freed goblin somewhere in these halls who knew the palace better than they did. And they had learned, which was the day's most durable lesson, that in this court a promise kept was worth more than a sword drawn, and that the difference between advocacy and interference was knowing which argument King Mago had not yet thought of himself.
