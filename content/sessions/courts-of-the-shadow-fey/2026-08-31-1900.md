The lower court of the Shadow Fey palace breathes differently from the halls above it — a cooler, dimmer air threaded with tallow smoke and the distant percussion of industry, where goblins and servants and minor functionaries go about the machinery that keeps a royal seat running. The party had bought themselves a foothold in this world, and on this day they set about turning it into something more.

Magnolia had made a promise to the kitchens, and whatever else might be said of the Circle of Dreams druid, she kept her word. She spent the morning coaxing goodberries from her magic — fifty of them, small and perfect, piled into a bowl and carried down to the head cook. The cook regarded them with the professional suspicion of someone who has been offered inferior ingredients before, then crushed one between two fingers, sniffed the pulp, and said nothing for a long moment. What followed was not praise but something better: the berries went directly into a soup pot, and the cook's demeanor shifted by precisely the amount that mattered in a place where every exchange is measured. Word moved through the service level as such things do, and Magnolia's standing moved with it.

Aldric's morning was less comfortable. He was summoned before King Mago to answer for the wardstone he had destroyed on the Shadow Road — the one his own fireball had reduced to rubble during the chaos of the crossing. Standing before the goblin king in a room that felt slightly too small for the weight of the moment, Aldric did not make excuses. He examined what remained of the stone, turned it over in his wizard's mind, and understood what he was looking at: a message ward, an alarm, something designed to carry information across distance. He repaired it. More than repaired it, if Ten-Pound Hammer's taciturn approval afterward meant anything. The goblin smith accepted the restored stone without ceremony, which in this court was the equivalent of a standing ovation.

Mago was not finished with him. The goblin king, who had his own reasons for keeping useful tools close, appointed Aldric Reader of the Lower Court — a position that sounded ceremonial until the first case landed in front of it. A goblin named Billoc had been brought up on a charge of theft. Billoc was mute, his only language the quick and precise grammar of his hands, and his hands were bound. The party saw it before anyone else in the room seemed to: the prisoner could not speak in his own defense because the court had silenced him with rope. Aldric made the argument plainly, without theatre. Judith and the others pressed the same point from different angles. There was no proof of the theft, and a man whose tongue lives in his fingers cannot be heard when you've tied his fingers behind his back. Mago, to his credit, listened. Billoc was freed, and when his hands came loose he found the party in the crowd with his eyes and held their gaze long enough for gratitude to be legible even without words.

Tam, meanwhile, had followed his instincts toward the stables. He had always understood animals better than politics, and the Black Prince's hunting dogs were excellent company — large, opinionated, immediately interested in the meat he'd had the foresight to bring. He spoke to them in the language that required no translation, and by the time Kolya the kennel master found him crouched in the hay with a hound's head across his knee, a rapport had been established that extended to the human as well. Kolya was not a man who trusted easily, but he respected someone who could make the dogs settle, and he talked. The Black Prince's hunt was a high court affair, the quarry conjured by Count Samus Hollowgrand's summoning arts; the great blue horse Lazulin waited in the largest stall at the end of the row. Tam filed all of it away. The hunt was a ladder, if he could find the right rung to grab.

Later, Judith and Tam drifted into the guard hall where the soldiers from Allosis kept their quarters — cots and whetted blades and the particular boredom of men posted far from home. Captain Memorethian's platoon was professional and dull, guardians of a castle they patrolled on rotation, not especially forthcoming and not especially hostile. They perked up when Tam alluded to Zobeck — the free city, soon perhaps to be something else — and accepted the framing without much scrutiny. Nothing of strategic value passed between them, but the visit established that the Allosis soldiers were a known quantity now, observed and noted, their routines slightly less opaque than they had been at dawn.

When the day's work was tallied, Aldric and Magnolia stood at status seven in the lower court, their names attached to something useful in Mago's eyes. Tam and Judith had reached five, a harder road ahead of them but a road nonetheless. The summer above them was still the summer, the Queen still on her throne, the clock of the turning season still running. Somewhere in the palace, Billoc was free. Somewhere in the stables, the blue horse waited. The path upward was not yet clear, but it was no longer invisible.
