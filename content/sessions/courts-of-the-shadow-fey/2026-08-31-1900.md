The lower palace breathed its own kind of air — older and dimmer than the galleries above, carrying the smell of tallow and iron and the particular sourness of a court that ran on fear and grudge. It was here, in the service warrens beneath the Summer Queen's glittering halls, that the party of Zobeck had chosen to root themselves, and by day's end they had begun to look less like strangers and more like fixtures. It was not a comfortable distinction, but in the Shadow Realm, comfort was a thing one gave up at the threshold.

The matter of the wardstone could not wait. Aldric Voss had broken it on the Shadow Road — shattered it, really, with the same fireball that had briefly ended his own life — and King Mago was not the sort of goblin king who forgot such things. Aldric went before him without pretense, inspected the damaged stone with the careful attention of a man who understood both magic and consequence, and recognized quickly what he had destroyed: a ward that served also as message and alarm, a small but genuine piece of the palace's nervous system. He did not apologize. He repaired it, and improved upon what had been there before. Ten-Pound Hammer examined the work with the thoroughness of a craftsman who takes insults to craftsmanship personally, then offered his approval without ceremony. The debt was settled in the only currency that mattered here: competence made visible.

Mago, apparently deciding that a wizard capable of repairing what he ruined was more useful than a wizard locked in a cell, appointed Aldric Reader of the Lower Court before the morning had passed. The first case was waiting. Billoc was a goblin who could not speak — had never spoken — and stood accused of theft with his hands bound behind his back, which was the same as standing accused with his mouth sewn shut. The injustice of it was the kind that courts like Mago's produced without malice, simply by failing to notice what they had done. Aldric noticed. He argued the thing plainly: a man whose only tongue is the motion of his fingers cannot be heard while those fingers are restrained, and a court that convicts the silenced has not heard anything at all. Mago listened, which was to his credit. He ordered the hands freed. Billoc signed what he had to say. There was no proof of theft, and everyone with eyes that worked could now see it. The goblin went free, and when he passed Aldric in the corridor afterward, he pressed his open palms together briefly in a gesture that needed no translation.

Below the courts, in the great kitchens that fed a fey palace across every shifting season, Magnolia Reed made good on a promise. She had given her word to the head cook, and a druid of her circle did not give her word lightly. Fifty goodberries, conjured at the cost of spells she would feel the absence of before nightfall, were presented and accepted. The cook crushed them into soup — some elaborate reduction that smelled of autumn meadows and honey gone strange — and the kitchen staff who tasted it remembered who had supplied the gift. In the lower court, where advancement was built from obligations honored and debts remembered, this was no small thing.

Tam Holloway found his route not through the corridors of power but through the stables, where the Black Prince's hunting hounds lived in better quarters than most of the court's lesser servants. He brought meat, and he brought the kind of patience that animals recognize before they recognize anything else. The dogs warmed to him, and through them he warmed to Kolya, the kennel master, a man who spent his days among creatures that did not lie and had developed a corresponding preference for people who behaved similarly. From Kolya, Tam learned the shape of the hunt: the quarry conjured by Count Samus Hollowgrand, a summoner of considerable reputation; the great blue horse Lazulin, who carried the Black Prince and no one else; the hunt itself, a high-court event that moved through the Shadow Realm's darker edges. It was a door, if they could find the invitation.

Judith, meanwhile, walked into the guard hall where Captain Memorethian's soldiers from Allosis kept their cots and their sharpened swords and their careful boredom. She spoke to them as soldiers speak to each other — about gear and rotations and the gap between what a post is supposed to be and what it actually is. She introduced herself as a representative of Zobeck, soon to be a satellite city, which produced a reaction that was neither hostile nor warm but was, at least, a reaction. The soldiers of Allosis had nothing to offer yet, but they had seen her face and heard her speak without flinching, and in a palace where everyone was measuring everyone else, that was a kind of currency too.

They gathered that evening in the lower palace's shadowed corridors, four people from a occupied city navigating a court that operated on rules older than any of them, and took stock of where they stood. Aldric and Magnolia, through competence and promise-keeping, had climbed to the seventh rank of the lower court. Tam and Judith held the fifth, earned through steadiness and the slow work of being present without incident. Above them, somewhere in the palace's bright and dangerous upper reaches, the Summer Queen's court continued its long season, and the clock that governed none of them — the Shadow Realm's own turning — moved quietly toward winter. They had not yet found their path to her. But they had found, perhaps, the people who might eventually show them one.
