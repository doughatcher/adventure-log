The lower court of the Shadow Fey palace smelled of tallow and old stone, its service corridors alive with the scuffle of goblin feet and the distant clang of kitchens working through the endless twilight that passed for morning in this realm. The party had learned by now that rank was the only currency that bought anything here, and they had each chosen their ground carefully for the day's work.

Magnolia had made a promise to the kitchens, and she kept it. Fifty goodberries, conjured from the last green memory of the living world, were pressed one by one into the hands of the head cook, who received them without ceremony and proceeded to crush every one into a copper pot of broth. The druid watched her spell slots rendered into soup without complaint. It was the keeping of the promise that mattered, not the form it took, and the lower court marked the difference between those who honored their word and those who let it dissolve like morning frost. Her standing rose accordingly.

Aldric's reckoning came from above. King Mago summoned him before the court to answer for the wardstone his fireball had shattered on the Shadow Road during the crossing — a stone that had served, he realized upon inspection, as both message-ward and alarm, the kind of thing that courts do not appreciate being destroyed by accident or otherwise. He neither deflected blame nor made excuses. Instead he examined the damage with a wizard's precision, bent his craft to the problem, and returned the stone improved beyond its original condition. Ten-Pound Hammer, the goblin smith, ran his thick fingers across the repair and gave a slow nod. The debt was acknowledged settled. Mago, watching all of this with the unhurried attention of a ruler who had learned to distinguish useful people from useless ones, appointed Aldric Reader of the Lower Court before the session was done.

The appointment proved immediately consequential. A case was already waiting — Billoc, a goblin who could not speak, whose hands were his only language, and who stood accused of theft with those hands bound behind his back. The party saw the problem before the advocates finished speaking. A man who can only answer in signs cannot answer at all when his hands are tied. Aldric pressed the point before Mago with the quiet precision of someone who understood that argument, in this court, was a form of power. The king listened. Billoc's hands were freed, the signs were read, the proof of theft was found wanting, and the goblin walked out of that hall into whatever passed for liberty in the lower court. He paused at the threshold long enough to look back at the party with something that was neither gratitude nor its performance but the real, exhausted thing.

While the rest of the party worked within the court's formal structures, Tam went to the stables. He had a nose for the edges of things — the places where rank mattered less than presence and patience — and the kennel runs behind the great stable block were exactly that kind of place. The Black Prince's hunting hounds were large and ill-disposed toward strangers, and Tam addressed both problems with meat and the kind of quiet address that animals understand before words do. By the time he met Kolya, the kennel master, the dogs had already decided he was acceptable, which was credential enough. Kolya was not a man who gave his trust cheaply, but he gave information to someone who clearly loved the animals in his care. The great blue horse Lazulin was the Black Prince's own mount. The hunt itself was a high-court event, its quarry conjured by the summoner Count Samus Hollowgrand. Getting anywhere near it required invitation. Getting the invitation was another problem entirely, but Tam had made a beginning.

Judith, meanwhile, walked into the guard hall where Captain Memorethian's soldiers from Allosis kept their cots and their patrols and their careful boredom. She offered them her sharpened blades to admire and a certain easy manner that suggested she belonged wherever she happened to be standing. The soldiers were not unfriendly — merely incurious about anything that did not threaten them — and she learned their rhythms without learning much else. The walls, the patrols, the shape of this level of the palace: it was the kind of reconnaissance that would not matter until suddenly it did.

When the twilight that served as evening settled over the lower court, the four of them took stock of where they stood. Aldric and Magnolia had climbed to a status that the court treated as meaningful. Tam and Judith held lower ground but had laid foundations — a kennel master who might become a guide, a hall of soldiers who would at least recognize their faces. The hunt, the Queen, the contracting window of Summer: all of it still lay above them, behind gates that rank alone would open. They had earned two days. They would need considerably more.
