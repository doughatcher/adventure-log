The lower palace breathed differently than the halls above — a quieter, grittier air, thick with tallow smoke and the smell of iron and hay. It was here, in the service corridors and lesser courts of King Mago's domain, that the party from Zobeck spent the first full day truly learning the shape of the shadow court's hierarchy. Not by forcing their way upward, but by doing the work the court required and letting the work speak for itself.

Magnolia had made a promise to the kitchens, and she kept it. She poured herself into the task with the patient deliberateness that had always marked her craft, spending spell after spell until she had conjured fifty goodberries into existence. The head cook received them without ceremony, crushed them into a great simmering pot, and said nothing more than was necessary — but the court noticed. Promises kept have weight in the shadow realm, and the druid's standing rose accordingly.

Aldric's reckoning came from an unexpected quarter. King Mago summoned him over the wardstone he had shattered with his own fireball on the Shadow Road — that near-suicidal casting that had killed him once already and left damage in its wake. He stood before the stone and studied it carefully, teasing apart what it had been: not merely a marker but a message ward, an alarm threaded with purpose. Rather than explain or apologize, he repaired it, and improved it, threading his magic through the fractures until what emerged was stronger than what had broken. Ten-Pound Hammer inspected the work with a craftsman's eye and accepted it. King Mago noted the result, and before long Aldric found himself standing in the Reader's role at a hearing — a mute goblin named Billoc, hands bound, accused of theft, unable to speak a word in his own defense. It was Aldric who recognized it first, and the party who made it plain: a man whose only voice lives in his hands cannot defend himself with them chained. Mago was persuaded. Billoc was freed. He signed his thanks and slipped away into the warrens of the lower court — grateful, somewhere, and perhaps useful yet.

Tam took a different path to the same end. He found the stables and the kennels, carrying meat and carrying patience, and the Black Prince's hunting dogs warmed to him the way animals do to someone who means it. Kolya the kennel master watched and did not throw him out, which was a form of welcome. Through careful conversation Tam learned the shape of the hunt — the great blue horse Lazulin, the quarry conjured by Count Sammas Hallowgrin, the high-court pageantry of the Black Prince's chase. It was not a door open to him yet. But he had found the door and noted where it stood.

Judith, meanwhile, walked the guard hall and took the measure of its soldiers — Shadow Fey from Allosis, under the command of a captain named Memorethian, garrisoned in a hall of cots and honed edges. She spoke easily with them, admired their sharpened swords, and learned what she could, which was not much. They were present, they were real, and they slept where they patrolled. A company of soldiers is a thing worth knowing about, even when they tell you very little. She filed the knowledge away and said nothing unnecessary.

When the accounting was done at the end of that long first day, Aldric and Magnolia each stood at status seven — the Reader's appointment and a kitchen's worth of kept promises between them. Tam and Judith held at five. The gap was not a judgment, merely a map. The path to the Summer Queen still ran through the court above, and the court above still demanded more than arrival to earn. The hunt was one road. Billoc, wherever he had gone, might be another. The season continued to turn.
