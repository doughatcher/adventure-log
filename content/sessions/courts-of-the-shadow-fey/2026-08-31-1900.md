The lower court of the Shadow Fey palace smelled of tallow and old stone, of unfamiliar cookery and the faint metallic edge that clings to places where magic is old and near the surface. The party had spent their first full day within its corridors not seeking audience with queens or princes, but doing the quieter, harder work of becoming people the court could not easily ignore.

Magnolia had made a promise to the kitchens, and she kept it. Fifty goodberries, coaxed from her druidic reserves, were delivered to the head cook, who received them without ceremony and immediately crushed them into a soup that would feed half the service level before the day was out. There was no fanfare in it. That, the court seemed to understand, was precisely the point. A promise kept without an audience is still a promise kept, and in a realm where bargains are the bedrock of all things, it counted.

Aldric's reckoning came from above, summoned before King Mago on account of the wardstone he had broken with his fireball during the crossing of the Shadow Road. He stood before the goblin king, examined the damaged stone with the careful eyes of a diviner, and saw at once what it was — a message ward, an alarm, something meant to listen and to speak across distances. He did not argue or deflect. He repaired it, and repaired it better than it had been. Ten-Pound Hammer examined the work and found it sound. Mago, satisfied or perhaps simply impressed by the lack of excuses, did not stop there. He named Aldric Reader of the Lower Court and put him immediately to use. The case was a mute goblin named Billoc, accused of theft, standing before the court with his hands bound behind him and no means to speak in his own defense. Aldric recognized the cruelty of it before anyone else gave it voice — a creature whose only language was his hands, silenced by the very rope meant to restrain him. He argued the point plainly: there was no proof of theft, and a man who cannot speak cannot be fairly tried. Mago listened. Billoc's hands were freed, his signs translated, and he walked out of the court with his life and his small dignity intact. He looked back once before he disappeared into the corridor, and the gratitude on his face needed no translation at all.

While Aldric stood before the throne and Magnolia fed the service staff, Tam followed his instincts toward the stables. The Black Prince's hunting dogs were large, well-kept, and immediately interested in the meat he had thought to bring. He spoke to them in the old way, the way his training as a Fey Wanderer had made second nature, and they accepted him with the uncomplicated warmth that dogs offer when they have decided they approve. Kolya, the kennel master, was harder to win over but not impossible — a practical man who respected practical knowledge, and Tam knew enough about animals to earn at least his attention. Kolya named Lazulin, the Black Prince's great blue horse, and spoke of the hunt as though it were the most important event in the palace calendar, which it very likely was. The quarry was conjured, Tam learned, by a summoner called Count Samus Hollowgrand. The hunt itself was a high-court affair, and entry to it would not come cheap. But the door, at least, had a name on it now.

Judith, for her part, moved through the guard hall and took its measure. The soldiers there were Shadow Fey from Allosis, under a captain named Memorethian — a disciplined platoon quartered in their hall like soldiers anywhere, sleeping in cots, sharpening swords, walking the walls. She engaged them with the easy confidence of someone who knows that soldiers respond to directness, offered to show her prowess with a blade, and when the initial impression landed poorly she recovered with a deception smooth enough to leave them thinking better of her than the evidence quite warranted. She learned little of substance, but she was remembered, and in the lower court, being remembered is not nothing.

By the time the day's work was done, the company had taken stock of what they had. Aldric and Magnolia had climbed to status seven — the wizard through accountability and advocacy, the druid through the simpler grace of keeping her word. Tam and Judith stood at five, which was not nothing either. The court above them remained in session, the Summer Queen still holding the season's authority while the Winter King's claim grew quietly in the dark beyond the palace walls. The path upward was not yet clear, but its outlines had begun to emerge: a kennel master who did not dislike them, a freed goblin who owed them something, a hunt that offered status to those bold enough to ride it, and a seasonal clock that did not wait for anyone to be ready.
