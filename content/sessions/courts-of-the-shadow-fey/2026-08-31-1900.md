The lower corridors of the Shadow Fey palace smelled of tallow smoke and old stone, and the party moved through them with the careful deliberateness of people who had learned that carelessness here carried a price. They had arrived at this court with nothing but names and nerve, and the work of earning something more had begun in earnest.

Aldric Voss was the first called to account. Word had reached King Mago that the wardstone on the Shadow Road had not simply been damaged in passing — it had been damaged by a fireball, which is to say by intention, which is to say by Aldric. The wizard stood before the goblin king and the stone both, examining the fractured ward with the practiced eye of someone trying to determine whether the problem was solvable before the consequences arrived. It had functioned as a message ward, an alarm threaded through the road's architecture, and he had shattered it without knowing what it was. He said nothing to excuse himself. Instead he repaired it, and improved it, and when Ten-Pound Hammer examined the result and gave his flat-faced approval, the king's attention shifted from punishment to opportunity. By the time Aldric left that audience, he had been made Reader of the Lower Court — a title that sounded ceremonial until he understood what it required.

His first case was a goblin named Billoc, mute and accused of theft, hauled before Mago with his hands bound behind him. The accusation was simple. The defense was impossible — not because Billoc was guilty, but because Billoc could not speak, and his hands were the only language he had. Aldric recognized it before the others named it aloud. He argued for the hands to be freed. The party pressed the point together, noting the absence of any real evidence, and Mago, whatever else he was, proved willing to be persuaded when the argument was sound. Billoc was released. He turned at the door, signed something brief and unmistakable, and was gone into the warren of the lower court. It was a small mercy in a place that did not deal in many, and it would not be forgotten.

While Aldric navigated the courtroom, Magnolia Reed moved toward the kitchens with something more fragrant in mind. She had made a promise, and the Shadow Fey remembered promises whether or not the one who made them did. Fifty goodberries, each one a compressed measure of her craft and her will, delivered to the head cook who crushed them into a soup that carried the faint warmth of living magic. The court noticed. The court rewarded her for it. There are hierarchies built on blood and conquest, and there are hierarchies built on who keeps their word — and in the lower court, at least, Magnolia had located a rung worth climbing.

Tam Holloway found his ladder in the stables. He had heard enough about dueling to understand that the route upward through combat was a genuine road, but one that cost you everything you'd built the moment you lost. He preferred longer odds to worse ones, and the dogs in the kennel were easier to read than most courtiers he'd met. He came in with meat and patience and the rare gift of knowing how to speak to animals without condescension, and the Black Prince's hunting hounds responded the way hunting hounds do when someone finally treats them like individuals — with loyalty that bypassed the formalities entirely. Kolya, the kennel master, was watchful at first and warming by the end. He named Lazulin, the great blue horse, and spoke of the hunt in the way men speak of things they are proud to serve: the quarry conjured by Count Samus Hollowgrand, the riders, the spectacle of it. The hunt was a high-court event, which meant it was a door, and Tam had just found someone who might know how to knock on it.

The guard hall yielded less, in the end, though it cost Judith some restraint to find that out. The soldiers inside were from Allosis — Shadow Fey in armor, under a captain named Memorethian, doing the work soldiers have always done in places like this: patrolling, sharpening swords, sleeping in shifts, and asking no more questions of their posting than their posting asked of them. Judith had walked in looking for leverage and left with a name and a geography. That was not nothing. But it was not yet useful, and she filed it alongside everything else the lower court had offered: information waiting to become advantage.

By the time the corridors had dimmed further and the day's obligations were discharged, the tally was as follows — Aldric and Magnolia at the seventh rank of court status, Tam and Judith at the fifth. The Summer Court still held session somewhere above them, its music audible through the stone on certain quiet hours. The seasonal clock continued its slow, inexorable turn. They had traded well for a single day's work, and Billoc was free, and the dogs would know Tam's scent, and the wardstone stood better than it had before Aldric broke it. In the Shadow Fey court, these things were not small. They were, in fact, exactly how it was done.
