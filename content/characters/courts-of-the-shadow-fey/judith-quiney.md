The name is doing a lot of work and it is doing all of it correctly. A human rogue who moves like someone who has thought carefully about when to use a blade and when to use a word, and who has arrived at the conclusion that both have their place and the choice is mostly about timing. When the Shadow Fey bard turned Magnolia's hand against the party, Judith killed the bard. That decision — fast, clean, correctly prioritized — says more about her than most conversations would.

In the Shadow Fey guard hall, Judith's instinct was to perform rather than persuade. Swords out, showing the soldiers what she could do, trying to establish credibility through visible competence. The first attempt landed poorly — shiny blades with no blood are not impressive to soldiers who have seen actual combat, and the guards said as much. She did not argue the point. She pivoted, found the thread of a deception instead, and pulled it hard enough to bring the whole encounter back from awkward. Twenty-four on the check. The guards ended the conversation satisfied, and Judith ended it at rank five.

There is a recurring shape to how she operates: lead with the thing that ought to work, absorb the failure without breaking stride, find the angle that actually works, and close. The swashbuckler's toolkit is built for exactly this rhythm — performance, reversal, improvisation, flourish. The Shadow Fey court, for all its alienness, is a place that rewards exactly that kind of adaptability. Judith has not yet found the room she cannot talk or cut her way into. The upper court will test whether that record holds.
